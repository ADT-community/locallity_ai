Fall armyworm is a major pest of maize across Africa and can also feed on many other plants. Farmers first learn to recognize it: moths lay egg masses on leaves, young larvae feed in the whorl and leave characteristic window-pane feeding marks and sawdust-like frass, while larger larvae hide in the whorl or between the ear and the stalk. Regular scouting from early vegetative growth onwards is the basis of management, because young larvae are much easier to control than large ones.

The guide promotes integrated pest management rather than routine spraying. Promising options include using varieties that are less susceptible, planting at the right time, maintaining healthy and fertile soils, and increasing diversity on the farm. Intercropping maize with legumes and the push-pull system, in which a repellent plant such as Desmodium is intercropped and an attractive border crop such as Napier grass is planted around the field, reduce infestation and support natural enemies such as parasitic wasps, ants and birds.

Mechanical and local controls include hand-picking and crushing egg masses and larvae where infestation is low, and the careful use of locally available materials such as ash, soil, sand, lime, soap, salt or oil, applied directly to the whorl. Botanical preparations made from locally available plants are described, but their preparation and dose need to be correct. Where intervention is needed, biopesticides based on naturally occurring pathogens are preferred. If chemical pesticides are used, they must be approved, dosed correctly, and handled with protective equipment, observing the harvest interval.

Farmer Field Schools are used to test these options side by side in the field, compare damaged and undamaged plants, count larvae, and keep records of costs and yields. Community-wide monitoring and data sharing make management more effective than acting on isolated farms.
