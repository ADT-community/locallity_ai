Groundnut yields in Africa are often below one tonne per hectare, but the crop can reach two to three tonnes or more with good practices. This guide from the Africa Soil Health Consortium explains the requirements of groundnut and how to fit it into intercrops and rotations.

Groundnut grows best in deep, loose, well-drained sandy or sandy-loam soils without compaction layers, because the pegs must enter the soil and the pods must be pulled out at harvest. Heavy clay soils and waterlogging are unsuitable. The soil should be light coloured and slightly acidic to neutral, with a pH around 5.5 to 7.0; groundnut does not tolerate saline soils. If a handful of moist soil can be rolled into a stable ribbon, the soil has too much clay.

Prepare a deep seedbed, breaking any hardpan and incorporating crop residues into the top soil. Apply lime if the soil is acidic and phosphorus fertilizer before planting, since most African groundnut soils are phosphorus deficient. Plant at the recommended spacing on flat beds or ridges; ridges help where drainage is poor. Groundnut should not follow groundnut in successive seasons. Rotate it every two to five years with maize, sorghum or millet, which benefit from the nitrogen it fixes, and avoid rotations with soybean, tobacco, cotton or tomatoes because of soil-borne diseases.

Water is needed most during emergence and during flowering, pegging and pod development. Where rainfall is high, use ridges; where water is scarce, use tied ridges to conserve moisture. Keep the crop weed-free, watch for leaf spot diseases and nematodes, and harvest when the pods are mature. Dry the pods well before shelling and store the kernels in dry conditions to avoid mould and aflatoxin contamination.
