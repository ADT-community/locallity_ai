Cassava is often called a zero-input crop, but good yields still depend on choosing the right land, variety, spacing and planting time. The training manual explains that the zero-input method avoids fertilizers and herbicides and instead encourages biological activity in the soil. It works best on land with adequate organic matter. Practice minimum tillage on sandy soils to conserve organic matter and moisture and reduce erosion; on shallow or hard soils, make ridges or mounds so that each plant has more topsoil.

Use improved varieties with the highest and most stable yield in the local area. Select healthy stems, cut them correctly and plant them at the recommended spacing and time. Keep the field weed-free during the first months, because young cassava suffers badly from weed competition; later, the canopy shades most weeds. Cassava/maize and cassava/legume intercrops make better use of land, reduce erosion and lower the risk of crop loss.

Harvest roots when they have reached a suitable size and maturity, usually several months after planting depending on the variety. Lift them carefully to avoid damage, since cut or bruised roots deteriorate quickly. Remove the roots from the field promptly and avoid leaving them exposed to sun and rain.

Fresh cassava roots have a very short shelf life and can begin to rot within a few days. Transport them quickly to the market or processing site, keep them in a cool shaded place, and process them into dried chips, flour or other products if storage is needed. The manual describes simple processing operations, hygiene and drying practices that reduce losses and improve the quality and market value of cassava products for smallholder farmers and processors.
