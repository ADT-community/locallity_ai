This study from IITA and partners, conducted in Malawi, looks at how to improve cassava yields, soil health, incomes and climate resilience on small farms. Farmland is shrinking, soils are depleted, few crops are grown and suitable varieties are often unavailable. Researchers tested different nutrient combinations and intercropping arrangements, especially alternating rows of cassava and cowpea, across three agro-ecological zones and later on farmers' fields in seven districts.

The most promising treatment was cassava intercropped with cowpea variety Sudan 1 in alternating single rows, with 60 kg of nitrogen per hectare for cassava split into two applications and 30 kg of nitrogen per hectare for cowpea. This system gave higher grain and storage-root yields, better land-use efficiency and greater profitability than farmers' traditional practice. Sudan 1 also out-yielded the alternative cowpea variety IT82E-16, showing that variety choice matters in an intercrop.

The practical message for farmers is that cassava and cowpea can be grown together successfully when the rows are arranged properly, the varieties are well matched and nutrients are applied in modest, split doses placed where the roots can use them. Intercropping diversifies the harvest, gives an earlier grain crop and helps restore soil health through the legume.

The technology was approved for release by Malawi's Agricultural Technology Clearing Committee. As with any recommendation, farmers should check which varieties and fertilizer rates are currently advised for their own area, since results depend on local soils and rainfall. Local extension services can provide that detail.
