Cassava is a staple food for millions of people in sub-Saharan Africa and can produce acceptable yields on poor soils with few purchased inputs. Farmer Field Schools help groups of farmers learn by doing: they set up small trials in their own fields, compare local and improved practices, observe pest and disease development through the season, and discuss the results in regular group meetings.

The resource material covers the cassava crop cycle from selecting healthy planting material to harvest and processing. Farmers learn to choose stems from healthy, high-yielding plants, to cut and plant them correctly, and to space plants so that the canopy closes at the right time. They compare practices such as minimum tillage, mulching, intercropping with grain legumes, and the use of compost or manure. Intercropping cassava with cowpea or maize can give an earlier harvest, reduce erosion, suppress weeds and spread the risk of crop failure.

Field exercises focus on identifying cassava diseases such as cassava mosaic disease and cassava brown streak disease, recognizing the symptoms early, and using healthy planting material to limit spread. Participants also learn to identify major pests and beneficial organisms, to estimate yield, and to keep simple records of labour, inputs and harvest.

The facilitator's guide emphasizes that the farmer, not the instructor, generates the knowledge: each season's observations are used to decide what to change in the next season. This makes recommendations locally appropriate and increases the likelihood that farmers will adopt practices that genuinely improve yields, food security and income in their own fields.
