Cassava gives low yields when soils are exhausted and fields are cleaned by burning. The Save and Grow approach shows how smallholders can raise yields while protecting the soil. Three practices are central: minimum or zero tillage, keeping the soil covered with mulch or cover crops, and diversifying the farm with intercrops and rotations. Planting cassava without tillage on degraded land may give lower yields in the first years, but as soil health recovers, untilled land can produce high yields at lower cost.

Mulch and cover crops suppress weeds and improve soil structure. Cassava grown in association, sequence or rotation with other crops increases income per unit area and reduces the risk of total crop loss. Intercropping with grain legumes such as cowpea or groundnut provides extra food and nitrogen, while protective hedgerows reduce erosion. Rotating cassava with legumes and cereals helps restore soil fertility.

Healthy planting material is essential. Stems should come from vigorous, disease-free plants, be cut correctly, and be stored and planted so that they do not dry out. Planting at the recommended spacing and at the right time for the local rainy season gives the crop a good start. Where soils are shallow or hard, ridges or mounds increase the volume of soil available to the roots.

Balanced applications of mineral fertilizer, combined with manure, compost and crop residues, can raise yields substantially, but nutrients removed in the harvested roots must be replaced. Pests and diseases are best managed through resistant varieties, healthy planting material, biological control agents and good field hygiene rather than routine insecticide use. The guide is intended for extension workers and farmers who want to intensify cassava production without degrading the land.
